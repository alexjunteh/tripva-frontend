Why Two Weeks in Thailand

Thailand is one of the few countries where two weeks feels like exactly the right amount of time. Not so short that you are rushing between highlights, not so long that the travel fatigue sets in. In fourteen days you can experience three entirely different Thailands: the chaotic, food-obsessed sprawl of Bangkok; the temple-studded mountains and hill-tribe culture of the north around Chiang Mai; and the limestone karsts, coral reefs, and white-sand beaches of the Andaman coast. Each region has its own rhythm, its own cuisine, and its own reason to exist on your itinerary.

This route moves north to south — Bangkok to Chiang Mai by overnight train, Chiang Mai to Krabi by flight, and then island-hopping by longtail boat and ferry. The geography works in your favour: domestic flights in Thailand are cheap (often under 2,000 THB / $55 USD one-way), the train network is surprisingly comfortable, and ferry connections between the southern islands run like clockwork in high season. You will never spend more than half a day in transit between legs.

Two weeks also gives you permission to slow down. The biggest mistake first-time visitors make is trying to cover too much ground — cramming in Phuket, Koh Samui, Pai, and Sukhothai on top of Bangkok and Chiang Mai. That is a different trip, and a more exhausting one. This itinerary deliberately chooses depth over breadth: three full days in Bangkok instead of one, three days in Chiang Mai with time for a cooking class and an elephant sanctuary, and almost a full week on the coast split between mainland Railay and one island. You will leave Thailand feeling like you actually experienced it, not like you photographed it from a bus window.

Days 1-3: Bangkok

Bangkok rewards those who arrive without a rigid plan and let the city pull them in whatever direction it wants. Three days is enough to see the essential temples, eat your way through the street food capital of the world, and get a genuine feel for why 22 million people live in this magnificent, maddening city.

Day 1: Temples and the River

Wat Phra Kaew and the Grand Palace should be your first stop, and you should arrive at 8:30am when the gates open — by 10am the tour groups make it oppressive. The Grand Palace complex is Thailand's most sacred site: the Emerald Buddha sits inside Wat Phra Kaew, a 66-centimetre jade figure dressed in gold seasonal robes by the King himself. Entry is 500 THB ($14 USD), which includes admission to several throne halls and the Coins and Royal Decorations Museum. Dress code is strict — long trousers or skirt below the knee, covered shoulders — and enforced at the entrance. If you forget, sarong wraps are available for rent outside the gate for 200 THB.

Walk south along the river to Wat Pho, home of the 46-metre reclining Buddha. The gold-plated figure fills the entire vihara, and the detail in the mother-of-pearl inlaid feet is extraordinary. Entry is 300 THB ($8 USD). Wat Pho is also the birthplace of traditional Thai massage — the on-site massage school offers one-hour sessions for 480 THB ($13 USD) and they are genuinely good, not a tourist trap.

Cross the Chao Phraya River by ferry (4 THB) to Wat Arun, the Temple of Dawn. The 70-metre central prang is covered in thousands of pieces of Chinese porcelain and coloured glass that catch the light differently throughout the day. Climbing the steep stairs to the terraces is worth it for the river views. Entry is 100 THB ($3 USD). The best photographs of Wat Arun, however, come from across the river at sunset — keep that in mind for a later evening.

In the evening, take a guided street food tour through Chinatown (Yaowarat). This is where Bangkok's food culture reaches its peak — grilled seafood on Yaowarat Road, the legendary Jay Fai (Michelin-starred street food — queue early or accept a two-hour wait), bird's nest soup shops, and mango sticky rice vendors on every corner. A self-guided walk works too, but a knowledgeable guide will steer you to stalls you would never find alone. Budget 500-1,000 THB ($14-28 USD) for an evening of eating.

Day 2: Markets and Modern Bangkok

Start at Chatuchak Weekend Market (Saturdays and Sundays only — if your Day 2 is a weekday, swap this to your Day 3). Chatuchak is the largest outdoor market in the world: over 15,000 stalls across 35 acres, selling everything from vintage clothing to handmade ceramics to live orchids. Go early (9am) before the heat becomes unbearable. Sections 2-4 for home decor, sections 17-19 for antiques, and the food perimeter for some of Bangkok's best coconut ice cream and pad thai. Budget 2-3 hours.

Afternoon: take the BTS Skytrain to Jim Thompson House (200 THB / $6 USD), a beautiful teak house museum built by the American silk magnate who revived Thailand's silk industry before mysteriously disappearing in Malaysia in 1967. The guided tour takes 40 minutes and the surrounding garden is a quiet counterpoint to the city outside.

For sunset, head to a rooftop bar. Sky Bar at Lebua (from The Hangover Part II) has the most famous view but charges 600+ THB per cocktail. Octave at the Marriott Sukhumvit offers a nearly identical panorama at 350-450 THB per drink. Either way, watching Bangkok's skyline light up from 50+ floors is a non-negotiable experience.

Day 3: Floating Market and Neighbourhood Deep-Dive

Take a morning trip to Amphawa Floating Market (about 90 minutes southwest of central Bangkok). Unlike the tourist-overrun Damnoen Saduak, Amphawa is where Thai families actually go on weekends. Vendors paddle wooden boats piled with grilled seafood, noodle soups, and coconut desserts. The market runs Friday-Sunday evenings and Saturday-Sunday mornings. Hire a private taxi or join a day tour (from 1,500 THB / $42 USD per person). If Amphawa's timing does not work, Khlong Lat Mayom is a smaller, more local floating market open on weekends and accessible by taxi.

Spend the afternoon in the old town neighbourhoods. Explore Talat Noi, a Portuguese-Chinese-Thai quarter with street art, century-old shophouses, and hidden cafes. Walk the Chao Phraya promenade. Visit the Museum of Siam (300 THB / $8 USD) for an interactive and genuinely entertaining history of Thai identity — one of the best museums in Southeast Asia, and almost empty on weekdays.

Bangkok Accommodation

  • Budget: Hostels on Khao San Road or in Silom — 300-600 THB/night ($8-17 USD). NapPark Hostel and Lub d Silom are consistently well-reviewed.
  • Mid-range: Hotels around Sukhumvit Soi 11 or near BTS stations — 1,500-3,000 THB/night ($42-83 USD). Aloft Bangkok Sukhumvit 11 and The Quarter Ari are solid picks.
  • Luxury: Riverside hotels with Chao Phraya views — 6,000-15,000 THB/night ($166-416 USD). The Peninsula Bangkok, Mandarin Oriental, and Capella Bangkok are all genuinely world-class.

Day 4: Overnight Train to Chiang Mai

This is the single best travel experience in Thailand that most visitors skip in favour of a one-hour flight. The overnight sleeper train from Bangkok to Chiang Mai departs from Hua Lamphong (or the newer Krung Thep Aphiwat Central Terminal — check your ticket) in the early evening and arrives in Chiang Mai around 7-8am the next morning. The journey is roughly 12-14 hours, depending on the service.

Book a second-class sleeper with air conditioning — this is the sweet spot. You get a fold-down bunk with a curtain for privacy, clean sheets, a pillow, and enough space to sleep comfortably. The upper bunk is slightly cheaper (around 800 THB / $22 USD) but the lower bunk (around 1,000 THB / $28 USD) has a window and more headroom. First class offers a private two-berth cabin for approximately 1,500 THB ($42 USD) per person — worth it for couples. Third class has seats only (no beds) and costs around 270 THB ($7.50 USD), but sleeping upright for 13 hours is not recommended.

Book your ticket 30-60 days in advance through 12Go Asia — the sleeper berths on popular evening departures sell out, especially in high season (November to February). The train leaves Bangkok as the sun sets over the city, and you wake up to green mountains and rice paddies rolling past your window. There is a dining car serving basic Thai food (pad thai, fried rice, curries — 60-120 THB per dish), or you can load up on snacks and beer from the 7-Eleven at the station before boarding.

If the overnight train does not appeal to you, budget airlines (AirAsia, Nok Air, Thai Lion Air) fly Bangkok to Chiang Mai for 1,200-2,500 THB ($33-69 USD) one-way, and the flight takes just over an hour. But seriously — take the train at least one way. It is one of those travel experiences that becomes a memory, not just a means of getting between cities.

Thailand Transport Tips
  • Trains: Book at 12go.asia — official tickets with e-ticket delivery. Second-class sleeper is the best value.
  • Domestic flights: AirAsia and Nok Air are the budget carriers. Book 2-4 weeks ahead. Hand luggage only saves 300-500 THB.
  • Ferries: Book via 12Go for southern island routes. Lomprayah and Tigerline are the most reliable operators.
  • Local transport: Grab (Southeast Asia's Uber) works perfectly in Bangkok and Chiang Mai. Red songthaews (shared pickup trucks) in Chiang Mai cost 30 THB per ride within the old city.
  • Scooter rental: 200-300 THB/day in Chiang Mai and the islands. International driving permit required legally, though rarely checked. Always wear a helmet — enforcement has increased and fines are 500 THB.

Days 5-7: Chiang Mai

Chiang Mai is the antithesis of Bangkok — smaller, cooler (both in temperature and temperament), surrounded by forested mountains, and built around a square old city bounded by a moat and crumbling walls. If Bangkok is where Thailand hustles, Chiang Mai is where it exhales. Three days here lets you cover the essential temples, get into the mountains, and still have time for the experiences that make Chiang Mai unique — a cooking class, an ethical elephant encounter, and the night bazaar.

Day 5: Temples of the Old City and Doi Suthep

Start inside the moat with Wat Chedi Luang, a 14th-century temple with a massive partially-ruined chedi (pagoda) that was once the tallest structure in the Lanna kingdom. The scale of the ruin is impressive — an earthquake in 1545 toppled the upper section, and the remaining brick-and-stucco structure stands at about 60 metres, roughly two-thirds its original height. Entry is free (donation appreciated).

Walk five minutes north to Wat Phra Singh, the most revered temple in Chiang Mai. The Lai Kham chapel in the rear houses the Phra Singh Buddha, a serene bronze figure in the classic Lanna style. The chapel walls are covered in original 19th-century murals depicting daily life in old Chiang Mai — look closely for scenes of market trading, courtship, and village festivals. Free entry.

In the afternoon, drive or take a songthaew (shared red truck, 60 THB each way from the old city) up the winding road to Doi Suthep, the mountain temple that overlooks the city from 1,070 metres. Climb the 309-step naga (serpent) staircase to the golden chedi at the summit. On a clear day, the terrace offers panoramic views over Chiang Mai and the surrounding valley. Arrive by 4pm and stay for the evening chanting ceremony at 5:30pm — watching the monks chant as the sun drops behind the mountains is one of those moments that makes the whole trip. Entry is 30 THB ($0.85 USD).

Day 6: Elephant Sanctuary and Night Bazaar

The morning should be spent at an ethical elephant sanctuary. This is important: do not ride elephants. The riding industry causes significant physical and psychological harm to the animals. Instead, visit a sanctuary where rescued elephants roam in semi-natural environments and you observe, feed, and walk with them — no riding, no chains, no performances. Elephant Nature Park (founded by Lek Chailert) is the gold standard — a half-day visit costs approximately 2,500 THB ($69 USD) and includes transport from the city, lunch, and several hours with the elephants. Elephant Jungle Sanctuary is another well-regarded option at around 2,200 THB ($61 USD). Book at least a week in advance in high season.

Return to the city by mid-afternoon and rest before heading to the Night Bazaar (daily, but busiest on weekends). The main Night Bazaar on Chang Klan Road runs every evening from around 6pm and sells everything from hill-tribe textiles to hand-carved soap flowers to knock-off designer goods. For a more authentic experience, visit the Sunday Walking Street (Ratchadamnoen Road) — the entire road closes to traffic and becomes a kilometre-long market with local artisans, street performers, and some of the best khao soi (Chiang Mai's signature curry noodle soup) you will find anywhere. Saturday Walking Street on Wualai Road is equally good with thinner crowds.

Budget 200-500 THB for night bazaar food (multiple small dishes is the correct strategy) and however much you want for souvenirs. Handwoven textiles and hill-tribe silver jewellery are the best-quality purchases — they are handmade locally and you will not find equivalent quality at these prices elsewhere.

Day 7: Thai Cooking Class and Free Afternoon

A Thai cooking class is the single best activity in Chiang Mai. Half-day classes (usually morning) start with a guided tour of a local market where you learn to identify Thai herbs, chillies, and produce, then move to a kitchen (often an outdoor pavilion surrounded by gardens) where you cook 4-5 dishes: typically pad thai, a curry paste from scratch, tom yum or tom kha soup, green or red curry, and mango sticky rice. Search Chiang Mai cooking classes on GetYourGuide — prices range from 900-1,500 THB ($25-42 USD). Thai Farm Cooking School, Mama Noi, and Asia Scenic are all excellent.

The afternoon is yours. Options: rent a scooter and ride to the Sticky Waterfalls (Bua Tong — limestone formations you can walk up barefoot, about 70 km north of the city), visit the MAIIAM Contemporary Art Museum (150 THB / $4 USD — a world-class collection of Thai modern art in a mirror-tiled building), explore the cafe scene in the trendy Nimmanhaemin neighbourhood, or simply walk the old city moat and catch a traditional Thai massage (200-300 THB / $6-8 USD for one hour — half the price of Bangkok).

Chiang Mai Accommodation

  • Budget: Guesthouses inside the old city moat — 400-800 THB/night ($11-22 USD). Julie Guesthouse and Deejai Backpackers are reliable.
  • Mid-range: Boutique hotels in the old city or Nimmanhaemin — 1,200-2,500 THB/night ($33-69 USD). BED Nimman and de Lanna Hotel have excellent locations and design.
  • Luxury: Resort properties on the city outskirts — 5,000-12,000 THB/night ($139-333 USD). Four Seasons Chiang Mai (rice paddy setting) and Dhara Dhevi (Lanna palace compound) are both extraordinary.

Day 8: Fly to Krabi

This is a travel day, and the logistics are straightforward. Fly from Chiang Mai (CNX) to Krabi (KBV) — direct flights operate daily on AirAsia and Thai Lion Air. Flight time is approximately 2 hours. Book 2-4 weeks ahead for the best fares: expect 1,500-3,000 THB ($42-83 USD) one-way. Morning departures get you to Krabi by early afternoon with the whole day still ahead of you. Search Chiang Mai to Krabi flights on Kiwi to compare times and prices.

From Krabi Airport, your destination is Ao Nang — the main beach town on the mainland and the launch point for longtail boats to Railay Beach. A shared minivan from the airport costs 150 THB ($4 USD); a private taxi is 600-800 THB ($17-22 USD). The drive takes 30-40 minutes.

If you arrive early enough, take a longtail boat from Ao Nang to Railay Beach (100-150 THB per person, 15-minute ride) and spend the afternoon settling in. If you arrive late, stay the night in Ao Nang (affordable and convenient) and head to Railay fresh in the morning. The longtail boats stop running after dark — last boats depart around 6pm in the dry season, earlier in monsoon season.

Days 9-11: Railay Beach & Four Islands

Railay is not an island, but it might as well be. A peninsula of sheer limestone cliffs cuts it off from the mainland entirely — there are no roads in, only boats. This geographical isolation means Railay has the feel of a remote island paradise while being a 15-minute boat ride from the mainland. It is, without qualification, one of the most beautiful places in Thailand.

Day 9: Railay Peninsula Exploration

Railay West is the main beach — a crescent of white sand between two 100-metre-tall limestone walls, with turquoise water and longtail boats bobbing in the shallows. This is the postcard. Railay East is muddier (mangrove coast) but has the cheaper accommodation and restaurants. A short path connects the two sides — the peninsula is only about 500 metres wide.

Hike to the Railay Viewpoint — a steep, rope-assisted scramble through jungle to a lookout over the entire peninsula. The climb takes 20-30 minutes and is genuinely challenging in parts (wear proper shoes, not flip-flops), but the panorama from the top — both beaches, the cliffs, the Andaman Sea stretching to the horizon — is breathtaking. Continue past the viewpoint and descend to Phra Nang Cave Beach, a hidden beach tucked at the base of a cliff with a cave shrine decorated with carved wooden phalluses (a fertility shrine — this is real, and locals take it seriously).

Afternoon: if you climb, Railay is one of the world's great rock-climbing destinations. The limestone cliffs offer routes for all levels, and several outfitters on the beach run half-day intro courses (1,500-2,000 THB / $42-55 USD including gear). Even if you have never climbed, the beginner routes on the Railay East wall are achievable and thrilling — climbing a vertical cliff face with the ocean below you is an unforgettable experience. Book rock climbing sessions on GetYourGuide.

Day 10: Four Islands Tour

The Four Islands tour is the signature day trip from Railay and Ao Nang. A longtail boat takes you to four locations: Chicken Island (named for a rock formation that unmistakably resembles a chicken's head), Tup Island (connected to Chicken Island by a sandbar you can walk at low tide), Poda Island (the best snorkelling of the four — clear water, coral, and tropical fish), and Phra Nang Cave Beach (if you have not already visited from Railay). Tours cost 800-1,200 THB ($22-33 USD) per person including lunch and snorkelling equipment, and run from 9am to about 3pm. Book through your guesthouse or any of the operators on the beach — prices are standardised.

The snorkelling on this tour varies from decent to excellent depending on the season and visibility. Between November and April, expect 10-15 metres of visibility and healthy coral at Poda. In the wetter months, visibility drops but the islands are still beautiful. Bring reef-safe sunscreen — the coral here is under pressure from tourism and chemical sunscreens accelerate bleaching.

Day 11: Kayaking or Rest Day

Rent a kayak (300-500 THB / $8-14 USD for a half day) and paddle along the base of the limestone cliffs. The coastline between Railay West and Phra Nang is dotted with sea caves, hidden lagoons, and tiny beaches inaccessible by foot. Paddle into the caves at low tide — some open into cathedral-like chambers with shafts of light cutting through holes in the ceiling. This is one of those experiences that feels like genuine exploration, even though the route is well-known to locals.

Alternatively, use Day 11 as a genuine rest day. You have been moving for ten days straight. Lie on Railay West with a book, get a beachside Thai massage (300-400 THB for an hour), eat barbecued fish from the evening beach vendors, and watch the sun set behind the cliffs. There is no guilt in doing nothing when the setting is this good.

Railay Accommodation

  • Budget: Bungalows on Railay East — 600-1,200 THB/night ($17-33 USD). Rapala Rock Wood Resort and Railay Garden View are basic but clean.
  • Mid-range: Railay West beachfront — 2,000-4,000 THB/night ($55-111 USD). Sand Sea Resort offers direct beach access.
  • Luxury: Rayavadee resort — 12,000-25,000 THB/night ($333-694 USD). Two-storey pavilions set in coconut groves between the beaches. One of Thailand's most celebrated hotels.

Days 12-14: Koh Phi Phi or Koh Lanta

For your final three days, you have a choice between two very different island experiences. Both are accessible by ferry from Krabi or Ao Nang. Your personality determines the right pick.

Option A: Koh Phi Phi — Energy and Spectacle

Koh Phi Phi is the island that put Thai beach tourism on the global map, and for good reason. The geography is absurd: two mountainous islands connected by a narrow sand isthmus, with turquoise bays on both sides. Maya Bay (made famous by the Leonardo DiCaprio film The Beach) reopened in 2022 with strict visitor caps — 300 visitors at a time, no boats in the bay, timed entry. The result is that Maya Bay is actually more beautiful now than it was during the mass-tourism era. The coral has recovered significantly, the water is clearer, and the beach is no longer wall-to-wall sunbeds. Day trips to Maya Bay from Phi Phi Don cost 400-800 THB ($11-22 USD).

Phi Phi is also a party island — Tonsai Village has bars, fire shows on the beach, and late-night energy every night. If you want nightlife as part of your Thailand experience, this is the place. If you want peace, Phi Phi has that too: Long Beach on the southeast coast is a 15-minute longtail ride from Tonsai and is dramatically quieter. Viewpoint hikes (30-45 minutes uphill) reward you with one of the most photographed panoramas in Southeast Asia — the twin bays from above.

Getting there: Ferries from Krabi to Koh Phi Phi on 12Go take about 1.5 hours and cost 450-650 THB ($12-18 USD) one-way. Two to three departures daily.

Option B: Koh Lanta — Calm and Character

Koh Lanta is the grown-up choice. A larger island with long, uncrowded beaches, a laid-back local community, excellent seafood restaurants, and none of the party-island atmosphere. If Phi Phi is the extrovert, Lanta is the introvert — and for many travellers, especially couples, it is the better final chapter.

Long Beach (Phra Ae) is the main tourist beach — three kilometres of golden sand with a handful of beach bars and restaurants. Klong Dao to the north is more family-friendly. The further south you go, the quieter it gets — Bamboo Beach and Kantiang Bay on the southern coast are genuinely empty most days.

Spend a day exploring by scooter (250-300 THB/day). Ride to Lanta Old Town on the east coast — a charming wooden-shophouse village built on stilts over the water, with Chinese-Malay-Thai architectural influences, small art galleries, and excellent coffee. The Mu Ko Lanta National Park at the southern tip of the island has a lighthouse, a rocky beach, and short hiking trails through old-growth forest. Snorkelling day trips to Koh Rok — an uninhabited national park island with pristine coral — depart from Lanta and cost around 1,500 THB ($42 USD) including lunch. The coral at Koh Rok is some of the best in the Andaman Sea.

Getting there: Ferries and minivan-ferry combos from Krabi to Koh Lanta on 12Go take 2-2.5 hours and cost 400-600 THB ($11-17 USD). Alternatively, a vehicle ferry crosses from the mainland (Lanta is close enough to drive to via two short ferry crossings).

Island Accommodation (Either Island)

  • Budget: Fan bungalows or hostels — 400-900 THB/night ($11-25 USD). Phi Phi: Blanco Beach Bar. Lanta: Bee Bee Bungalows.
  • Mid-range: Air-con beach bungalows — 1,500-3,500 THB/night ($42-97 USD). Phi Phi: ChaoKoh Phi Phi Hotel. Lanta: Long Beach Chalet.
  • Luxury: Boutique resorts — 5,000-15,000 THB/night ($139-416 USD). Phi Phi: Zeavola Resort (north tip, adults-focused). Lanta: Pimalai Resort & Spa (Kantiang Bay, exceptional).

When to Visit Thailand

Thailand's climate splits into three seasons, and the timing of your trip significantly affects both the weather and the cost. Here is what each window looks like:

November to February is the sweet spot — temperatures in the low 30s Celsius during the day, cooler evenings (especially in Chiang Mai where it can drop to 15C at night), minimal rain, and calm seas in the south. This is peak season, which means higher accommodation prices (20-40% above low season), crowded temples and beaches, and the need to book trains, ferries, and popular hotels well in advance. December and January are the busiest months. If you can travel in November or February, you get nearly identical weather with somewhat thinner crowds.

March to May is Thailand's hot season. Temperatures in Bangkok regularly exceed 38C and the humidity is brutal. Chiang Mai is even worse — the combination of heat and agricultural burning (the infamous "burning season" in March-April) creates a thick haze that obscures the mountains and drops air quality to unhealthy levels. The coast is still viable in March, but by April the heat is oppressive everywhere. The upside: shoulder-season prices, fewer tourists, and Songkran (Thai New Year, April 13-15) — the world's biggest water fight — is an extraordinary cultural experience if you can handle the heat.

June to October is rainy season. "Rainy" in Thailand does not mean constant rain — it typically means short, heavy afternoon downpours followed by sunshine. Mornings are usually clear. The Andaman coast (Krabi, Phi Phi, Lanta) gets the worst of it, with rougher seas and some ferry cancellations in September and October. The Gulf coast (Koh Samui, Koh Phangan, Koh Tao) has opposite weather patterns — June to September is actually pleasant on the Gulf side. Budget travellers love this season: prices drop 30-50% across the board, popular sites are emptier, and the landscapes are lush and green. Just bring a good rain jacket and accept that one afternoon in three will be wet.

Budget Breakdown: $800 vs $2,000

Thailand is one of the best-value travel destinations in the world, but the gap between budget and comfort spending is surprisingly wide. Here is what two weeks actually costs at each level:

Budget Traveller: ~$800 USD (28,800 THB) for 14 Days

  • Accommodation: Hostels and fan bungalows — average 450 THB/night x 13 nights = 5,850 THB ($162)
  • Food: Street food and local restaurants — 400 THB/day x 14 days = 5,600 THB ($155)
  • Transport: 2nd-class train Bangkok-CM (1,000 THB), budget flight CM-Krabi (1,800 THB), ferries (1,200 THB), local transport (2,000 THB) = 6,000 THB ($166)
  • Activities: Temple entries, cooking class, Four Islands tour, viewpoint hikes = 4,000 THB ($111)
  • Miscellaneous: SIM card, laundry, insurance, incidentals = 3,350 THB ($93)
  • Total: ~24,800 THB (~$690 USD) — leaving buffer for extras. Tight but absolutely doable.

Comfort Traveller: ~$2,000 USD (72,000 THB) for 14 Days

  • Accommodation: Mid-range hotels and beach resorts — average 2,200 THB/night x 13 nights = 28,600 THB ($794)
  • Food: Mix of street food and restaurants with occasional upscale meals — 800 THB/day x 14 days = 11,200 THB ($311)
  • Transport: 1st-class sleeper train (1,500 THB), mid-tier flight CM-Krabi (2,200 THB), ferries and speedboats (1,800 THB), Grab rides (3,000 THB) = 8,500 THB ($236)
  • Activities: Elephant sanctuary, cooking class, rock climbing, Four Islands, Maya Bay, guided tours, rooftop bars = 10,000 THB ($278)
  • Miscellaneous: Cocktails, shopping, spa treatments, tips, insurance = 9,700 THB ($269)
  • Total: ~68,000 THB (~$1,889 USD) — comfortable without being extravagant.

Neither budget includes international flights. Both assume sharing a room with a travel partner (solo travellers add 30-50% for accommodation). The $800 budget requires discipline — eating primarily street food, taking local transport, and choosing fan rooms over air-con — but Thailand is one of the few countries where budget travel does not feel like deprivation. The street food is world-class, the local transport is an experience in itself, and a 500 THB bungalow on Koh Lanta can have an ocean view that a $300/night hotel elsewhere would envy.

Photo Spots — Three Best Shots

  • 1
    Wat Arun at Sunset from Across the River
    The most iconic photograph in Bangkok. Position yourself on the Maharaj Pier side of the Chao Phraya River (several riverside restaurants and bars offer unobstructed views) and shoot Wat Arun's central prang as the sun drops behind it. The temple's porcelain-encrusted surface catches the warm light, transitioning from gold to deep amber to silhouette as the sky shifts through the entire spectrum. In the foreground, longtail boats and river ferries cross the frame, adding motion and scale. Use a 70-200mm lens to compress the temple against the sky, or go wide (24mm) to include the river and boats. A tripod allows you to shoot into blue hour when the temple is illuminated — the warm lights against the deep blue sky is the definitive Bangkok image.
    Best time: 5:30-7:00pm. Eagle Nest Bar and Sala Rattanakosin rooftop both offer elevated, unobstructed angles. Arrive 30 minutes before sunset for the best seat.
  • 2
    Doi Suthep Temple Above the Clouds
    On mornings when low cloud sits in the Chiang Mai valley, Doi Suthep's golden chedi rises above a sea of white fog with mountain ridges cutting through in the distance. This shot requires luck (cloud inversions are most common November-January between 6:30-8:00am) but when it happens, the result is one of the most dramatic temple photographs in Asia. Even without the cloud layer, the view from the terrace over Chiang Mai city with the golden chedi in the foreground is exceptional. Shoot with a wide angle to capture both the ornate golden stupa and the vast valley below, or use a telephoto to isolate the chedi tip against layers of mountain ridges.
    Best time: 6:30-8:00am for cloud inversions. Drive up before the temple opens (gates open at 6am) — the mountain road is empty and the light is at its best.
  • 3
    Railay Beach from the Viewpoint
    The Railay Viewpoint hike delivers a shot that defines southern Thailand: two perfect bays (Railay West and Phra Nang) separated by a narrow jungle ridge, framed by vertical limestone cliffs, with turquoise water stretching to scattered islands on the horizon. Longtail boats appear as tiny coloured dashes on the water, giving the image an almost miniature-model quality. The hike is steep and muddy in places — wear shoes you do not mind getting dirty — but the 360-degree panorama at the top is the single most comprehensive view of Andaman coastal geography you will find without a drone. Shoot in the morning when the sun lights both bays evenly, or late afternoon when the western bay glows gold.
    Best time: 7:00-9:00am or 4:00-5:30pm. The hike takes 20-30 minutes up. Bring water — there is no shade at the top and the heat builds quickly.

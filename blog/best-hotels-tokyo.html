Tokyo Neighbourhoods Overview — Which Area Is Right for You

Tokyo is not one city. It is dozens of cities stacked on top of each other, connected by the most efficient rail network on Earth. The neighbourhood you choose as your base will shape your entire trip more than almost any other decision, because Tokyo's districts are not gradual transitions — they are hard cuts. Walk ten minutes from Shinjuku's screaming neon canyon of bars and you are in Shinjuku Gyoen, a garden so immaculate and silent that you can hear individual leaves falling. Take the Yamanote Line two stops from the designer boutiques of Omotesando and you step out into the electronic chaos of Akihabara, where every building is an arcade, a manga shop, or a maid cafe. Tokyo does not blend. It juxtaposes.

This guide covers the six neighbourhoods that make the most sense for visitors, followed by specific hotel recommendations at every price point, from capsule pods that cost less than a good dinner to suites where the bathtub looks out over the Imperial Palace. I have stayed in all six districts across nine trips over fourteen years, and what follows is honest advice about which one matches your trip.

The single most important rule for choosing where to stay in Tokyo: stay within a 5-minute walk of a JR Yamanote Line station. The Yamanote Line is a loop that circles central Tokyo, connecting every major district in 60 minutes. If you are on the Yamanote Line, you are 30 minutes or less from everything that matters. Stray too far from it, and Tokyo's vastness will cost you hours in transfers and underground corridors.

Before we dig into each neighbourhood, here is the quick decision framework. First trip to Tokyo? Stay in Shinjuku. The station is overwhelming but the access is unbeatable, the hotel stock is enormous, and you can walk to Golden Gai, Kabukicho, and Shinjuku Gyoen. Shopping and youth culture? Shibuya or Harajuku. Traditional Japan on a budget? Asakusa. Money is not a constraint? Ginza. Nightlife is the priority? Roppongi. Anime, games, and electronics? Akihabara. There is no wrong answer because the trains make everything accessible, but your base neighbourhood determines what you stumble into at midnight when you are wandering without a plan, and those unplanned moments are where the best travel memories live.

Shinjuku — Best for First-Timers

Shinjuku is where Tokyo's intensity is turned up to maximum volume. Shinjuku Station processes 3.5 million passengers per day — more than any other railway station on Earth — and the surrounding neighbourhood packs more hotels, restaurants, bars, department stores, and entertainment venues into a few square kilometres than most cities contain in total. It is chaotic, it is overwhelming on the first day, and it is the best base in Tokyo for first-time visitors because the transport connections are unmatched.

The West Exit is the business and hotel district. This is where you find the major hotel towers — Hilton, Hyatt Regency, Keio Plaza, and the Park Hyatt (of Lost in Translation fame). The streets are wide, the buildings are corporate-sleek, and the atmosphere is relatively calm compared to the east side. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building observation decks are here, offering free 360-degree views from the 45th floor — one of the best free activities in the city.

The East Exit is where Tokyo gets loud. Kabukicho is Japan's largest entertainment district — an exploding grid of neon signs, host clubs, izakayas, karaoke towers, and Robot Restaurant (a seizure of lights, robots, and J-pop that defies description and costs 8,500 JPY / $57 USD). Just south of Kabukicho, Golden Gai is a labyrinth of over 200 tiny bars crammed into six narrow alleys, each seating 6-12 people, each with its own theme, music, and personality. Some are jazz bars. Some are punk bars. Some are bars where the mama-san will make you sing before she serves you. Golden Gai is one of the most singular drinking experiences in the world, and it is reason enough to base yourself in Shinjuku.

Shinjuku Gyoen, a 10-minute walk south of the station, is a 144-acre garden that combines Japanese, English, and French landscape styles. Entry is 500 JPY ($3.35 USD) and it is the best place in Tokyo to escape sensory overload. During cherry blossom season (late March-early April) it is one of the most beautiful spots in the country.

Hotels in Shinjuku range from business hotels at 8,000 JPY ($54 USD) per night to the Park Hyatt at 80,000+ JPY ($535+ USD). The sweet spot is the cluster of mid-range hotels around Nishi-Shinjuku: Tokyu Stay Shinjuku, Hotel Gracery Shinjuku (with the Godzilla head on the roof), and Citadines Central Shinjuku all deliver clean, modern rooms for 15,000-25,000 JPY ($100-168 USD). Shinjuku Station survival tips
  • Follow the coloured lines on the floor. They lead to specific exits. Learn your hotel's nearest exit number before you arrive.
  • The East and West exits are a 10-minute underground walk apart. Do not assume you can nip between them quickly.
  • Google Maps works perfectly inside the station. Turn it on and follow the blue dot. This is not the time for pride.
  • Avoid rush hour (7:30-9:30am, 5:30-7:30pm). If you must travel during rush hour, let one or two trains pass and board the next one — the compression is real.

Shibuya — Best for Culture and Shopping

If Shinjuku is Tokyo turned up to eleven, Shibuya is Tokyo at its most stylish. The Shibuya Scramble Crossing — where up to 3,000 people cross simultaneously from five directions every time the lights change — is the single most iconic image of modern Tokyo, and the energy of standing in the middle of it as the crowd surges around you is genuinely electric. But Shibuya is much more than the crossing.

Walk north from the station and within 15 minutes you reach Harajuku, the centre of Japanese street fashion. Takeshita-dori is the famous pedestrian street of crepe shops, kawaii accessories, and vintage clothing stores, but the real fashion finds are on the quieter Cat Street parallel to it, where independent Japanese designers and second-hand luxury stores line a tree-shaded former river path. Continue north and you hit Omotesando, often called Tokyo's Champs-Elysees — a boulevard of architectural statement buildings housing flagships from every luxury brand, plus the stunning Nezu Museum with its collection of pre-modern Japanese art and a hidden garden that feels impossibly serene for central Tokyo (entry 1,300 JPY / $8.70 USD).

Shibuya's food scene is excellent. The basement floors of the station's new Shibuya Scramble Square building contain some of the best depachika (department store basement food halls) in Tokyo, with counters selling everything from wagyu bento to matcha pastries to fresh sashimi boxes. The upper floors house Shibuya Sky, a rooftop observation deck at 230 metres with open-air terraces and glass floor panels — entry is 2,000 JPY ($13.40 USD) and the sunset views rival any paid observation deck in the city.

Hotels in Shibuya tend to be slightly pricier than Shinjuku for equivalent quality. The Shibuya Stream Excel Hotel Tokyu sits directly above the station with floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the scramble crossing — rooms from 20,000 JPY ($134 USD). Sequence Miyashita Park is a design-forward hotel built into Miyashita Park, with a rooftop bar and rooms starting at 18,000 JPY ($120 USD). Budget travellers should look at the Millennials Shibuya or The Share Hotels Lyuro for clean, design-conscious pods and rooms from 5,000 JPY ($33 USD).

Shibuya is the best base for travellers whose priorities are shopping, contemporary culture, and food. It is one stop from Shinjuku on the JR Yamanote Line, making everything else easily accessible. The main downside: the area immediately around the station is loud and crowded until midnight. If you want the Shibuya location without the chaos, look at hotels in Daikanyama or Nakameguro, two quieter residential neighbourhoods one stop south on the Tokyu Toyoko Line, known for independent coffee shops, bookstores, and some of Tokyo's best restaurants.

Asakusa — Best for Traditional Tokyo and Budget Stays

Asakusa is old Tokyo. While Shinjuku and Shibuya hurl themselves into the future, Asakusa holds onto the past with both hands. The neighbourhood centres on Senso-ji, Tokyo's oldest temple, founded in 645 AD — a full millennium before the city was even called Tokyo. The approach through the massive Kaminarimon (Thunder Gate) and down the 250-metre Nakamise-dori shopping street, lined with stalls selling traditional snacks, paper fans, wooden combs, and ningyo-yaki (doll-shaped cakes), is one of the most atmospheric walks in the city. Visit before 7am to experience Senso-ji in near-silence, with incense smoke drifting across the empty courtyard and the temple's red lacquer glowing in the early light.

Asakusa's streets retain the low-rise, tile-roofed character that most of Tokyo lost to post-war reconstruction. The lanes around the temple are filled with senbei (rice cracker) shops, tempura restaurants that have been operating since the Edo period, unagi (freshwater eel) specialists, and traditional ryokan inns with tatami floors and communal baths. The Sumida River runs along the eastern edge of the district, and the river promenade offers views of both the Tokyo Skytree (634 metres, Japan's tallest structure) and Philippe Starck's surreal golden-flame Asahi Beer Hall.

Asakusa is also the budget capital of central Tokyo. Because it sits on the eastern edge of the tourist map, slightly off the Yamanote Line (the nearest Yamanote station is Ueno, two stops north on the Ginza Line), hotel prices are 20-40% lower than Shinjuku or Shibuya. Budget guesthouses and hostels start at 3,000-5,000 JPY ($20-33 USD) per night. Traditional ryokans — where you sleep on futons on tatami mats and soak in a shared Japanese bath — start at 8,000 JPY ($54 USD). Mid-range business hotels like Richmond Hotel Premier Asakusa or The Gate Hotel Asakusa (which has a rooftop terrace with direct Skytree views) run 12,000-18,000 JPY ($80-120 USD).

The trade-off: Asakusa is quieter after dark, with far less nightlife than Shinjuku or Shibuya. The last trains to Asakusa run around midnight, and late-night taxi rides from central Tokyo cost 3,000-5,000 JPY ($20-33 USD). If your trip is temple-focused, if you want a traditional Japanese stay, or if budget matters, Asakusa is the best choice. If nightlife and shopping are priorities, look elsewhere and visit Asakusa as a day trip.

For a unique Asakusa experience, book a guided walking tour that covers the temple grounds, hidden backstreet food stalls, and the history that the self-guided visit misses.

Ginza — Best for Luxury

Ginza is where Tokyo puts on a suit. This is the city's oldest and most prestigious shopping and entertainment district, where every major luxury brand operates a multi-storey flagship, where department stores like Mitsukoshi (founded in 1673) sell art and fashion with a level of service that makes Western retail look amateurish, and where the backstreet sushi counters casually serve some of the best fish on Earth.

The main drag, Chuo-dori, becomes a pedestrian promenade on weekend afternoons (noon-5pm on Saturdays and Sundays, April-September), and strolling it with the car-free boulevard stretching between gleaming storefronts is one of Tokyo's most pleasant urban experiences. The architectural highlights include the Ginza Six complex (a soaring atrium designed by Yoshio Taniguchi, who designed MoMA's renovation), the Mikimoto building (a shimmering curtain of aluminium and glass by Toyo Ito), and the Hermes tower (glass block facade that glows like a lantern at night).

But Ginza's real treasure is its food. The Ginza-Shiodome-Tsukiji corridor contains the highest concentration of Michelin stars per square kilometre on Earth. Sukiyabashi Jiro — the three-Michelin-star sushi counter featured in Jiro Dreams of Sushi — is here, though reservations require a concierge at a luxury Japanese hotel and the omakase runs 44,000 JPY ($295 USD). More accessible but equally exceptional counters include Sushi Saito (three stars, 33,000 JPY / $221 USD omakase) and Harutaka (two stars, 25,000 JPY / $168 USD). Even Ginza's casual options are extraordinary — the standing sushi bars in the basement floors of Ginza department stores serve excellent nigiri for 3,000-5,000 JPY ($20-33 USD).

Hotels in Ginza reflect the district's character: refined, understated, and expensive. The Bvlgari Hotel Tokyo occupies floors 40-45 of the Bvlgari Ginza Tower, with rooms from 120,000 JPY ($804 USD). The Hyatt Centric Ginza is a more accessible luxury option at 35,000-50,000 JPY ($235-335 USD), with a design-forward aesthetic and an excellent location steps from the main shopping streets. For mid-range options, Mitsui Garden Hotel Ginza Premier offers polished rooms with city views from 20,000 JPY ($134 USD).

Ginza is the right base if fine dining, high-end shopping, and quiet sophistication are your priorities. It is not the right base if you want nightlife, street food, or youth culture energy. Ginza is also the closest major district to Tsukiji Outer Market (the surviving street food market from the old Tsukiji fish market, now relocated to Toyosu but the outer market still operates with excellent seafood stalls and sushi breakfast counters).

Roppongi — Best for Nightlife and Museums

Roppongi has a split personality. After dark, the main strip is Tokyo's international party district — a dense corridor of clubs, bars, and restaurants where the crowd is a mix of expats, tourists, and Japanese night owls. The nightlife runs later here than almost anywhere else in Tokyo, with clubs like 1OAK, V2 Tokyo, and the long-running Roppongi Hills Club keeping doors open until 5am or later. Cover charges range from free to 5,000 JPY ($33 USD) depending on the night and venue.

But Roppongi's daytime identity is equally compelling. Roppongi Hills, the massive mixed-use complex developed by Mori Building Company, houses the Mori Art Museum on the 53rd floor — one of Asia's most important contemporary art institutions, with rotating exhibitions that consistently draw international attention. The admission (1,800 JPY / $12 USD) includes access to the Tokyo City View observation deck, which offers 360-degree panoramas from 250 metres. Across the district, Tokyo Midtown houses the Suntory Museum of Art (Japanese decorative arts) and the 21_21 Design Sight museum (contemporary design, founded by Issey Miyake and Tadao Ando). Within walking distance, the National Art Center Tokyo is Japan's largest exhibition space, housed in a stunning undulating glass building by Kisho Kurokawa — entry to the building is free, with exhibition-specific tickets typically 1,600-2,000 JPY ($10.70-13.40 USD).

Hotels in Roppongi lean upscale due to the business and embassy district overlap. The Grand Hyatt Tokyo inside Roppongi Hills is one of the city's finest hotels (rooms from 50,000 JPY / $335 USD), with a Nagomi Spa and the outstanding Oak Door steakhouse. The Ritz-Carlton Tokyo occupies the top floors of Tokyo Midtown, with rooms from 70,000 JPY ($469 USD) and views that extend to Mount Fuji on clear days. For more moderate budgets, Remm Roppongi and APA Hotel Roppongi offer compact but well-maintained rooms from 10,000-15,000 JPY ($67-100 USD).

A note of caution: Roppongi's nightlife strip has a reputation for aggressive touts, drink-spiking at disreputable establishments, and overcharging at certain bars (the "hospitality scam" where a bill arrives at 10x the expected amount). Stick to established venues, do not follow touts into unfamiliar bars, and check drink prices before ordering. The vast majority of Roppongi is perfectly safe, but the precautions are worth knowing.

Explore Roppongi's art and culture scene with a guided art museum tour that covers the Mori Art Museum, 21_21 Design Sight, and the National Art Center in a single afternoon.

Akihabara — Best for Anime and Gaming Culture

Akihabara is another planet. If anime, manga, video games, or electronics are even a passing interest, this district will consume an entire day of your trip and you will emerge blinking into reality carrying bags you did not plan to fill. Known as "Electric Town" since the post-war years when it became Japan's centre for electronics retail, Akihabara has evolved into the global epicentre of otaku (geek/fan) culture.

The main strip, Chuo-dori, is lined with multi-storey buildings dedicated to single fandoms. Yodobashi Camera Akiba is a nine-storey electronics megastore where you can buy everything from professional camera lenses to Japanese-only kitchen gadgets. Mandarake Complex is an eight-storey temple of second-hand manga, anime figures, vintage toys, and doujinshi (self-published comics). Super Potato is a retro gaming shrine — three floors of vintage game consoles, cartridges, and a top-floor arcade packed with 80s and 90s cabinets where you can play original Street Fighter II and Pac-Man machines for 100 JPY ($0.67 USD) per game.

The arcades deserve special mention. SEGA GiGO (multiple locations), Taito Station, and Hey! Hirose Entertainment Yard offer floor after floor of crane games (UFO catchers), rhythm games, fighting game cabinets, and photo booths (purikura). The crane game floors are dangerously addictive — each play costs 100-200 JPY and the prizes are high-quality anime figures, plush toys, and snacks. Budget at least 2,000 JPY ($13.40 USD) for crane games alone.

The maid cafe scene is quintessentially Akihabara. Waitresses in elaborate maid costumes serve omurice (omelette rice) with ketchup drawings, perform song-and-dance routines, and address you as "master" or "princess." It is surreal, wholesome, and a window into a subculture that exists nowhere else. @home Cafe and Cure Maid Cafe are the most established options. Entry is typically free, with a one-drink minimum (600-900 JPY / $4-6 USD) and food from 800-1,500 JPY ($5.35-10 USD).

Hotels in Akihabara are limited but growing. The area is better for a day visit than an overnight stay, but Dormy Inn Akihabara offers excellent mid-range rooms with a free rooftop onsen (hot spring bath) from 12,000 JPY ($80 USD). Nohga Hotel Akihabara is a design hotel with a craft beer bar and rooms from 18,000 JPY ($120 USD). Budget travellers can find capsule hotels and hostels from 3,500 JPY ($23 USD). Akihabara is two stops from Tokyo Station on the JR Yamanote Line, so even if you stay elsewhere, getting here takes minutes.

Budget Hotels and Capsule Hotels Worth Trying

Tokyo has a reputation for being expensive, and it certainly can be. But the city also has one of the most inventive budget accommodation scenes in the world. Japanese efficiency, space optimisation, and design consciousness mean that even the cheapest rooms in Tokyo are clean, well-maintained, and cleverer than budget rooms in most other cities.

Capsule Hotels — The Quintessential Tokyo Experience

Even if you can afford a regular hotel, you should spend at least one night in a capsule hotel. It is one of those experiences that is unique to Japan and tells you more about Japanese culture — the ingenious use of space, the communal bathing rituals, the quiet respect for shared environments — than any museum visit.

A capsule hotel gives you a sleeping pod (roughly 2m x 1m x 1.25m), a shared lounge, a shared bathroom with proper Japanese wash stations, and usually a communal onsen or large bath. Your pod has a mattress, pillow, blanket, a small TV, a reading light, an alarm clock, and a privacy curtain or rolling shutter. Luggage goes in a locker on a different floor. Most capsule hotels provide a yukata (cotton robe) and toiletries. Prices range from 3,000-5,000 JPY ($20-33 USD) per night.

The best capsule hotels in Tokyo have elevated the concept far beyond the salary-man crash pads of the 1980s:

  • Nine Hours Shinjuku — minimalist, design-focused capsule hotel with a clean aesthetic that looks like it was designed by Apple. Pods are sleek white cylinders with ambient lighting. From 4,500 JPY ($30 USD). Locations also in Shin-Osaka and near Narita Airport.
  • The Millennials Shibuya — upgraded pods with motorised beds, personal projector screens, and a co-working lounge. The communal spaces feel more hostel-lounge than budget-hotel-corridor. From 4,800 JPY ($32 USD).
  • Capsule Hotel Anshin Oyado Shinjuku — traditional-style capsule hotel with an onsen bath, sauna, and massage chairs. Popular with Japanese business travellers, which is always a quality indicator. From 3,800 JPY ($25 USD). Men only (a common capsule hotel restriction — check before booking).
  • First Cabin Tsukiji — "first class" capsule concept with larger-than-average pods modelled on airline first-class seats, complete with a roll-down privacy screen and a small desk area. From 5,500 JPY ($37 USD).

Budget Hotels and Hostels

Beyond capsule hotels, Tokyo's budget hotel scene includes business hotels (compact but perfectly functional rooms, typically 12-15 sqm, with private bathroom, desk, and all amenities) and a growing number of design-forward hostels that offer private rooms at guesthouse prices.

  • Toyoko Inn — Japan's largest business hotel chain, with locations near every major station. Rooms are small (12 sqm) but immaculately clean, with free rice-ball breakfast, coin laundry, and reliable Wi-Fi. From 6,000 JPY ($40 USD). Book direct on their website for the best rates.
  • APA Hotels — another major chain with locations everywhere. Rooms are notoriously tiny (9-11 sqm) but the quality is consistent and the locations are prime. From 7,000 JPY ($47 USD).
  • Unplan Shinjuku — a stylish hostel with private rooms from 8,000 JPY ($54 USD) and dorm beds from 3,500 JPY ($23 USD). The bar and common area attract a good social crowd without being a party hostel.
  • Nui. Hostel & Bar Lounge (Kuramae) — converted warehouse near Asakusa with a ground-floor bar-cafe, high ceilings, and an artsy atmosphere. Dorms from 3,000 JPY ($20 USD), private rooms from 9,000 JPY ($60 USD).

Budget accommodation tips
  • Book 2-3 months ahead for cherry blossom season (late March-mid April) and autumn colours (mid November-early December). Prices double and availability vanishes.
  • Check-in is typically 3pm, check-out 10am. Many hotels offer luggage storage before and after these times — use it.
  • Coin laundry is everywhere. A wash-and-dry cycle costs 300-600 JPY ($2-4 USD). Pack light and wash as you go.
  • Convenience store meals are legitimately excellent in Japan. A 7-Eleven onigiri (rice ball, 150 JPY / $1), a Family Mart bento (500 JPY / $3.35), and a Lawson egg sandwich (250 JPY / $1.67) will keep you fed for under $10/day.

Mid-Range Picks ($100-200/night)

The $100-200 USD range is the sweet spot for Tokyo hotels. At this price point you get rooms that are genuinely comfortable (not just functional), proper bathrooms with Japanese toilet technology that will ruin Western bathrooms for you forever, and locations within walking distance of major stations. Here are the properties I have stayed in or inspected and would confidently recommend:

Shinjuku

  • Hotel Gracery Shinjuku (15,000-22,000 JPY / $100-148 USD) — The one with the life-size Godzilla head peering over the 8th-floor terrace. Rooms are modern, well-sized for Tokyo, and the Kabukicho location means Golden Gai and the entertainment district are steps away. The Godzilla-view rooms cost a premium but are worth it once for the novelty.
  • Tokyu Stay Shinjuku (14,000-20,000 JPY / $94-134 USD) — What sets Tokyu Stay apart is the in-room washer-dryer and kitchenette. For stays of 3+ nights, this is the most practical mid-range choice in the district. Rooms are larger than typical business hotels at 18-22 sqm.
  • Citadines Central Shinjuku (18,000-26,000 JPY / $120-174 USD) — Serviced apartment-style rooms with full kitchens, living areas, and a residential feel. Excellent for families or stays of a week or more.

Shibuya / Harajuku

  • Sequence Miyashita Park (18,000-25,000 JPY / $120-168 USD) — Built into Shibuya's Miyashita Park with a rooftop bar and flexible check-in/check-out times (the late 8pm check-in / 2pm check-out is unusual and genuinely useful for jet-lagged travellers).
  • Mustard Hotel Shibuya (15,000-20,000 JPY / $100-134 USD) — Design hotel with a ground-floor cafe, small but stylish rooms, and a Shibuya backstreet location that is walkable to everything but away from the worst crowds.

Asakusa / East Tokyo

  • The Gate Hotel Asakusa (16,000-24,000 JPY / $107-161 USD) — The rooftop terrace has one of the best views in Tokyo: Senso-ji's pagoda in the foreground, Tokyo Skytree behind it, and the Sumida River to the right. The rooms are compact but the common areas and bar are outstanding.
  • Wired Hotel Asakusa (14,000-19,000 JPY / $94-127 USD) — A community-focused hotel with a ground-floor cafe-bar, a curated library, and an atmosphere that blurs the line between hotel and neighbourhood hangout. Excellent value for the quality.

Ginza / Tokyo Station

  • Mitsui Garden Hotel Ginza Premier (20,000-30,000 JPY / $134-201 USD) — Upper floors of a Ginza tower with city views, a sleek minimalist interior, and walking distance to Tsukiji Outer Market for sushi breakfast. The bath with a view rooms on the higher floors are exceptional value for the Ginza district.
  • Hotel Metropolitan Tokyo Marunouchi (22,000-28,000 JPY / $148-188 USD) — Directly connected to Tokyo Station, which matters more than you might think when you are carrying luggage and navigating the JR system. Clean, efficient, and well-located for day trips to Hakone, Kamakura, and Kyoto.

Luxury Picks ($300+/night)

Tokyo's luxury hotel market is, per dollar spent, the best in the world. The level of service, attention to detail, and design quality at Tokyo's top hotels exceeds comparable properties in London, Paris, or New York at often half the price. A $400/night room in Tokyo delivers what would cost $800-1,200 in Manhattan or Mayfair. The Japanese hospitality concept of omotenashi — anticipating and fulfilling a guest's needs before they are expressed — is not a marketing buzzword here. It is a cultural practice visible in every interaction, from the way your luggage appears in your room before you do, to the way the breakfast buffet rearranges itself based on the nationality mix of that morning's guests.

Park Hyatt Tokyo (Shinjuku)

The Lost in Translation hotel. The Park Hyatt occupies floors 39-52 of the Shinjuku Park Tower, and stepping into the 41st-floor lobby — a double-height space of bamboo screens, Egyptian stone, and floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the city — is still one of Tokyo's great arrival moments, 30+ years after the hotel opened. The New York Bar on the 52nd floor, where Bill Murray drank whisky while a jazz singer performed against a wall of city lights, is real, it is as good as the film suggests, and the cover charge (2,200 JPY / $14.75 USD after 8pm, waived if dining) is the best-value luxury experience in Tokyo. Rooms from 55,000 JPY ($368 USD). The 20-metre indoor pool with Mount Fuji views on clear days is exceptional.

Aman Tokyo (Otemachi)

Aman brought its signature minimalism to a 38-storey tower near the Imperial Palace, and the result is one of the most serene hotel experiences in any major city. The rooms start at 84 sqm — enormous by Tokyo standards — with washi paper screens, camphor wood detailing, and deep soaking tubs positioned beside floor-to-ceiling windows. The spa spans two floors and includes a traditional Japanese onsen. The lobby lounge, set in a vast double-height space with paper lantern-inspired lighting, serves a formal afternoon tea that rivals anything in London. Rooms from 120,000 JPY ($804 USD). Worth it if the budget allows — this is quiet, considered luxury at its finest.

The Peninsula Tokyo (Marunouchi)

Overlooking the Imperial Palace gardens and Hibiya Park, The Peninsula offers the most classically grand luxury experience in Tokyo. The rooms feature nail-head detailing, marble bathrooms with mood lighting, and a bedside control panel that manages everything from curtains to bath temperature. The location is perfect for Ginza shopping, Imperial Palace walks, and Tokyo Station access. The Peter restaurant on the 24th floor serves contemporary European cuisine with palace garden views. Rooms from 70,000 JPY ($469 USD). The Peninsula's afternoon tea, served in the lobby amid live string performances, is a Tokyo institution.

Hoshinoya Tokyo (Otemachi)

A vertical ryokan in a 17-storey tower, Hoshinoya Tokyo rejects the Western luxury hotel model entirely. There are no shoes past the entrance — you change into slippers in the lobby and leave the city behind. Each floor is a self-contained "unit" of six rooms sharing a communal living room (ochanoma) with tea and snacks. The rooftop onsen, fed by natural hot spring water pumped from 1,500 metres underground, has open-air baths where you soak naked under the Tokyo skyline. The kaiseki dinner is multi-course Japanese haute cuisine served in your room. Rooms from 80,000 JPY ($536 USD). This is the most uniquely Japanese luxury stay in Tokyo — nowhere else offers this combination of traditional ryokan hospitality in a modern urban setting.

The Ritz-Carlton Tokyo (Roppongi)

Occupying the top nine floors of the 53-storey Tokyo Midtown Tower, every room at The Ritz-Carlton Tokyo is above the 45th floor, guaranteeing unobstructed city views from every window. On clear days, Mount Fuji is visible from west-facing rooms. The club lounge on the 53rd floor serves five daily food presentations including a champagne-and-canape evening service with 360-degree panoramic views. The spa uses local Japanese ingredients — sake-based facials, hinoki cypress treatments. Rooms from 70,000 JPY ($469 USD).

Transport Tips — JR Pass, Suica, and Getting from the Airports

Tokyo's public transport system is the best in the world. Trains run on time to the second, stations are spotlessly clean, announcements are in Japanese and English, and the network covers every neighbourhood you would want to visit. But the system is also vast and initially confusing — Tokyo has 13 subway lines, multiple JR lines, and dozens of private railways, each operated by different companies with different ticketing. Here is what you actually need to know.

Suica / Pasmo Card — Get This Immediately

A Suica (or the functionally identical Pasmo) is a rechargeable IC card that works on virtually every train, subway, and bus in the Tokyo metropolitan area. You tap it on the reader at station gates and the fare is automatically deducted. It also works at convenience stores, vending machines, coin lockers, and many restaurants. Buy one from any JR ticket machine at the airport or any major station — the card costs 500 JPY ($3.35 USD, refundable deposit) and you load it with whatever amount you want. Budget 1,500-2,000 JPY ($10-13.40 USD) per day for transport if you are actively sightseeing.

Since mid-2023, physical Suica cards have been in limited supply due to a semiconductor shortage. The workaround: add a Suica to your Apple Wallet or Google Pay before you arrive. This works with any iPhone 8 or later, or any NFC-capable Android phone. The digital Suica functions identically to the physical card and can be topped up directly from your phone.

Japan Rail Pass (JR Pass)

The JR Pass is a multi-day unlimited pass for all JR trains nationwide, including the Shinkansen (bullet train). Within Tokyo, JR operates the Yamanote Line (the loop that connects all major districts), the Chuo Line (east-west through the centre), and several other useful lines. A 7-day JR Pass costs 50,000 JPY ($335 USD) as of 2026.

The JR Pass is only worth it if you are taking long-distance Shinkansen trips. A single Tokyo-Kyoto round trip on the Shinkansen costs 27,720 JPY ($186 USD), so if you are doing Tokyo-Kyoto-Osaka or Tokyo-Hiroshima during your trip, the pass pays for itself. For a Tokyo-only trip, the JR Pass is overkill — the Suica card is all you need. You can compare JR Pass prices and book train tickets on Rail Ninja to check whether the pass makes financial sense for your specific itinerary.

Getting from Narita Airport to Central Tokyo

Narita is 60-80km east of central Tokyo. There are four main options:

  • Narita Express (N'EX) — JR's airport express train, running directly to Tokyo Station (55 min), Shibuya (75 min), and Shinjuku (85 min). The standard fare is 3,250 JPY ($21.80 USD) one way, but JR sells a round-trip N'EX discount ticket for foreign passport holders at 5,000 JPY ($33.50 USD) — buy it at the JR counter in the arrivals hall. This is the best option for most travellers: fast, comfortable, direct, and covered by the JR Pass if you have one.
  • Skyliner — Keisei Railway's premium service to Ueno and Nippori stations (36 minutes, the fastest option). 2,520 JPY ($16.90 USD) one way. Best if your hotel is in Ueno, Asakusa, or anywhere on the east side of the Yamanote Line.
  • Access Express — Keisei's budget option, running the same route as the Skyliner but stopping at more stations (55-70 minutes). 1,270 JPY ($8.50 USD). The best value option — half the price of N'EX and only slightly slower.
  • Airport Limousine Bus — Direct buses to major hotels and stations. 3,200 JPY ($21.40 USD). Takes 85-120 minutes depending on traffic. Only worthwhile if the bus stops directly at your hotel.

Getting from Haneda Airport to Central Tokyo

Haneda is much closer — 15-20km south of central Tokyo. The Tokyo Monorail runs to Hamamatsucho Station (13 minutes, 500 JPY / $3.35 USD), where you connect to the Yamanote Line. The Keikyu Line runs to Shinagawa (11 minutes, 300 JPY / $2 USD), also on the Yamanote Line. Both are fast and cheap. Taxis from Haneda cost 5,000-8,000 JPY ($33-54 USD) to central Tokyo — reasonable for a group of 3-4 splitting the fare, especially late at night when trains stop running (last trains around midnight).

Transport tips for first-timers
  • Google Maps is the best Tokyo transit app. Enter your origin and destination, select the transit option, and it will show you every route, platform number, and transfer with live timing. It is more reliable than any dedicated transit app.
  • Last trains run around midnight. Check the last train time for your line before you go out. Missing it means a 5,000-10,000 JPY ($33-67 USD) taxi ride or finding a manga cafe to sleep in until the first train at 5am.
  • Rush hour is 7:30-9:30am. Trains are packed to 180% capacity. If you can, delay your morning until 10am — the difference is dramatic.
  • Stand on the left side of escalators (in Tokyo — Osaka is reversed). Walking is on the right.
  • Do not eat or talk on the phone on trains. It is considered rude. Texting and quiet conversation are fine.

Best Time to Visit Tokyo

Tokyo has four distinct seasons and each one transforms the city in ways that go beyond weather. Unlike tropical destinations where the main variable is rain, Tokyo's seasons change the entire visual character of the city, the food menus, the festivals, and the hotel prices.

Spring (March-May): Cherry blossom season — hanami — is Tokyo's most magical and most crowded period. The blossoms typically peak between late March and early April (exact dates vary yearly; check Japan Meteorological Corporation forecasts in February). During peak bloom, every park in Tokyo fills with picnicking groups sitting on blue tarps beneath pink canopies, and the atmosphere is one of the most joyful things you will experience anywhere. Shinjuku Gyoen, Ueno Park, Chidorigafuchi (the moat around the Imperial Palace), and the Meguro River in Nakameguro are the best viewing spots. Hotel prices rise 40-60% and availability disappears — book 3-4 months ahead. May is warmer, less crowded, and underrated.

Summer (June-August): June is rainy season (tsuyu), with persistent drizzle and high humidity. July and August are hot (30-35C / 86-95F) and muggy, and sightseeing during midday hours is exhausting. The upside: spectacular summer festivals — Sumida River Fireworks (late July, one of the largest fireworks displays in the world), Mitama Matsuri at Yasukuni Shrine (July, 30,000 illuminated paper lanterns), and neighbourhood bon odori dance festivals throughout August. Hotel prices drop 20-30% outside of festival weekends. Summer is the best time for budget travellers willing to tolerate the heat.

Autumn (September-November): The best time to visit Tokyo. September is still warm but the humidity breaks. October brings comfortable temperatures (15-22C / 59-72F) and clear skies. November delivers koyo — autumn foliage — when the city's gardens, parks, and temple grounds erupt in red, orange, and gold. Rikugien Garden and Koishikawa Korakuen offer nighttime illumination of their autumn maples that is among the most beautiful things in Tokyo. Crowds are smaller than cherry blossom season but still elevated in late November. Book 2-3 months ahead.

Winter (December-February): Cold (2-10C / 36-50F) but crisp, dry, and clear. Winter is when Tokyo's air is cleanest and Mount Fuji is most visible from the city — the luxury hotel observation decks and Shibuya Sky are at their best on winter mornings. December has Christmas illuminations throughout Omotesando, Roppongi, and Marunouchi that rival European cities. January 1-3 is hatsumode (first shrine visit of the year) when millions of Tokyoites visit Meiji Shrine and other temples in an extraordinary cultural spectacle. Hotel prices are at their lowest (except the New Year period). Winter is Tokyo's best-value season and the city is fully functional — it rarely snows in central Tokyo.
